# Presentation build

**Structure is the deliverable. Slides are a by-product.**

A structure exists to do two jobs at once: let the audience follow the argument and remember the message, and let the speaker stay calm, on point, and able to move between sections without groping for the link. A deck built before the structure does neither — it becomes the speaker's teleprompter and the audience's reading assignment.

So the order is fixed: **brief → section map → speaker script → slides.** Do not open a slide tool before Step 3 is written down.

---

## Step 1 — the brief (never skip, never guess)

Three questions. Ask them one at a time — this user prefers single questions — and do not proceed until all three have real answers.

1. **What is the goal of this presentation?** Not the topic. The goal. Inform, persuade, sell, teach, get a decision, get a budget approved.
2. **Who is in the room, and what do they already know?** Their role, their seniority, their prior exposure to the subject. This sets how much context to build and which jargon is free versus which must be earned.
3. **After it ends, what is the one thing they must remember?** One sentence. If it takes three, the presentation has no message yet — work it down to one before continuing.

Then settle the five constraints that change the shape of the structure:

- **Duration**, and whether it is hard-stopped.
- **Interaction level** — pure talk, Q&A at the end, questions throughout, or hands-on exercises.
- **Context** — conference stage, boardroom, Zoom, classroom, a slot inside someone else's agenda. Online means shorter sections and more frequent signposting; nobody can read the room through a grid of tiles.
- **Is there a product or system demo?** A demo changes the structure (see the Demo pattern in `references/structures.md`) and needs its own fallback plan.
- **What visual aids exist or are needed** — real screenshots, data, diagrams, video, physical artefacts.

Write the brief down as six short lines and show it back before building anything. Everything downstream is judged against line 3.

---

## Step 2 — pick the structure

Default to the **basic five-part structure** below. Switch to an alternative only when the situation in the table matches — the full patterns, with their section-by-section sequences, are in `references/structures.md`.

| Pattern | Use when | Sequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Basic** | Most talks, updates, teaching | Greeting → opening → body → conclusion → thanks & Q&A |
| **Demo** | Introducing a product or system | Value → need → problem → demo → how it scales |
| **Problem–solution** | Persuading someone to change | Problem → impact → solution → call to action |
| **Story** | Building emotion, holding attention | Context → challenge → journey → outcome → lesson |
| **Elimination** | Contested topic with several camps | Problem → the options → limits of each → recommendation |

The five parts of the basic structure:

**1. Greeting and introduction.** Who you are, the experience that makes you the right person for *this* subject, why it is worth their attention. Keep it short — the audience came for the content, not the CV.

**2. Opening — broad to specific.** Introduce the topic, state the problem or context, make the objective explicit, say what they will walk away with, preview the agenda in one line, and state the duration plus how to ask questions. This part's job is to create interest and tell them where they are going.

**3. Body.** Split into clear sections. Every single section must carry all four of these — a section missing one is not ready:

- One main point (one, not two)
- Evidence or an example that supports it
- What it means *for this audience*
- A one-sentence summary said out loud before moving on

Order the sections by whichever of these fits the material: **priority, theme, chronology, cause and effect, or problem and solution.**

While drafting each section, answer out loud: *"What am I saying here, and what does the audience need to understand from it?"* If the second half of the answer is vague, the section is decoration — cut it.

**4. Conclusion.** It must do more than repeat. Signal that the end is coming, restate the objective, summarise the main points, state what it all means, and give the next action. Opening and conclusion get more preparation time than anything else — they set how the talk is received and what survives afterwards.

**5. Thanks and Q&A.** Thank the audience and invite questions. If Q&A is the real substance of the session, the prepared part still has to be sharp enough to give the discussion something to stand on. Prepare answers to the five questions most likely to come — including the hostile one.

## Step 3 — the section map (the actual work product)

Before any slide exists, produce a table the user can correct in one pass:

| # | Section | Main point (1 sentence) | Evidence | So what, for them | Transition into next | Min |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Rules for filling it in:

- **Budget the time first, then fit the content.** Rough arithmetic that holds up: opening ≈ 10% of the slot, conclusion + Q&A ≈ 20%, body gets the rest. A 20-minute talk supports **three** body sections. 45 minutes supports four or five. More sections than that and the audience stops tracking where they are.
- **Every transition is written out as a sentence**, not left to improvisation. That sentence does four things: summarise what just finished, restate why it mattered, say what comes next, and name the link between the two. Transitions are where speakers lose the room and lose their own thread. Phrasings to reuse are in `references/structures.md`.
- **For a group presentation**, the outgoing speaker summarises their own part, introduces the next person by name, and says what that person will cover. Write those handovers into the map like any other transition.
- Mark which sections are **cuttable** if time runs short. Decide that now, calmly, not on stage.

Show the map and get it corrected **before** writing the script or the slides. Rework here costs minutes; rework after the deck is built costs hours.

---

## Step 4 — the speaker script

Produce a full script alongside the deck — not bullets. Per section: the opening line, the point stated plainly, the evidence with its numbers, the so-what, the summary sentence, the written transition, and the target minute mark. Verbatim wording for the first 60 seconds and the last 60 seconds; the middle can be written as speakable prose the speaker will naturally adapt.

Use `references/script-template.md` for the format, the timing column, and the Q&A prep block.

---

## Step 5 — slides

Slides support the spoken words; they do not replace them. Anything that must be *said* does not belong on the slide.

- **One slide, one idea.** Only the information that slide actually needs.
- **One or two images or diagrams instead of a paragraph.** If a slide is a wall of text, it is a document — send it as a document.
- **Big enough to read from the back row.** Guy Kawasaki's 10–20–30 is the reference point: ~10 slides, ~20 minutes, minimum 30pt type. It is a guide to calibrate against, not a law — a 3-hour workshop obviously breaks it. What does not bend is the type size.
- **Give the audience time to take a slide in.** A dense slide flashed for four seconds is worse than no slide.
- **Include an agenda slide and a summary slide.** They are the audience's map.
- Apply the user's brand via the `brand-context` skill. Never invent colours or fonts.
- Any chart follows the `dataviz` skill.

**Choosing the output channel** — all four are in use; pick by what happens to the deck next. Full mechanics for each in `references/slide-channels.md`.

| Channel | Pick it when |
|---|---|
| **Gamma** | Fastest path to a good-looking deck; the user will keep editing it online or share a link |
| **.pptx** | It must open in PowerPoint or Keynote, be emailed, or be handed to a client or an event organiser |
| **HTML Artifact** | Layout control matters, or the deck should live at a shareable URL and be presented from a browser |
| **Canva / Adobe Express** | A designer or the marketing team will take it further in the tool they already work in |

Ask which one if it is not obvious. Build the deck from the section map — **one slide per idea inside a section, never one slide per section.**

---

## Step 6 — pre-delivery check

- [ ] The one-sentence message from brief Q3 is recoverable from the conclusion alone
- [ ] Every body section has point / evidence / so-what / summary line
- [ ] Every transition is written, including group handovers
- [ ] Section count fits the duration (3 sections for ~20 min)
- [ ] Opening and conclusion are scripted word-for-word
- [ ] Agenda slide and summary slide present
- [ ] No slide the speaker would need to read aloud
- [ ] Type ≥ 30pt; checked at the size the room will actually see
- [ ] Demo has a fallback (recorded video or screenshots) if there is a demo
- [ ] Five likely questions prepared, including the hostile one
- [ ] Cuttable sections marked
- [ ] Timed out loud at least once against the budget
