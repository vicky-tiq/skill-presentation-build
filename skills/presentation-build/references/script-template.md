# Speaker script template

Deliver this as a separate markdown file next to the deck, not as slide notes only — the speaker rehearses from this.

---

## Header block

```
Talk:        <title>
Audience:    <who, what they already know>
Goal:        <inform / persuade / sell / teach / get a decision>
One message: <the single sentence they must remember>
Slot:        <minutes> · hard stop: <yes/no>
Context:     <stage / boardroom / Zoom / classroom>
Q&A:         <throughout / at the end>
```

---

## Per section

```
### [n] <Section name>            [target: 00:00 – 00:00]   slides <a–b>

POINT
<the one sentence this section exists to land>

SAY
<speakable prose — the evidence, the example, the numbers, in the order they
come out of your mouth. Not bullets. Read it aloud once and fix whatever your
tongue trips on.>

SO WHAT
<what this means for this specific audience>

SUMMARY LINE (say this out loud)
"<one sentence>"

TRANSITION
"<written-out sentence: summarise → why it mattered → what's next → the link>"

CUTTABLE: yes / no
```

---

## Word-for-word blocks

Two parts get scripted verbatim, because they decide how the talk is received and what survives it.

**First 60 seconds** — greeting, who you are, why this is worth their time, the objective, the agenda in one line, duration and Q&A rules.

**Last 60 seconds** — the end signal, the objective restated, the main points, what it means, the next action.

Write both out in full. Rehearse both until they do not need the paper.

---

## Q&A prep block

```
| Likely question | Short answer | Evidence to reach for |
|---|---|---|
```

Five questions minimum. One of them must be the hostile one — the objection the most sceptical person in the room is already forming. Prepare the answer for that one first; it is the one that gets asked.

Also prepare: **"I don't know"** with a follow-up commitment ("I don't have that number with me — I'll send it by Thursday"). It beats improvising a wrong figure in front of the person who knows the right one.

---

## Timing check

Rehearse out loud against the clock at least once, and record the actual minute marks beside the targets. Reading silently runs roughly 30% faster than speaking, so a script that "fits" on paper usually overruns. If it overruns, cut the sections already marked cuttable — do not speed up.
