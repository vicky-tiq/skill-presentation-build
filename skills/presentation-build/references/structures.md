# Structures, ordering, and transitions

## The five patterns in full

### Basic — the default

| Part | Contains | Share of time |
|---|---|---|
| Greeting & introduction | Who you are, relevant experience, why this matters to them | ~5% |
| Opening | Topic → problem/context → objective → what they get → agenda → duration & Q&A rules | ~10% |
| Body | 3–5 sections, each: one point + evidence + so-what + summary line | ~65% |
| Conclusion | Signal the end → restate objective → summarise → meaning → next action | ~10% |
| Thanks & Q&A | Thank, invite questions, handle them | ~10% |

Broad to specific in the opening; specific back to broad in the conclusion.

### Demo — introducing a product or system

`Value → need → problem → live demo → how it scales`

Lead with the value, not the feature list. Establish the need and the problem the audience recognises *before* anything is shown on screen, otherwise the demo is a tour of buttons. Close on scale: what this looks like at 10x the volume, or across the rest of the business.

Always prepare a fallback — a recorded screen capture or a screenshot sequence — and say up front that questions on the demo are welcome at the end, so the flow does not fragment.

### Problem–solution — persuading someone to change

`Problem → impact → solution → call to action`

The impact section is the one people skip and the one that does the persuading: quantify what the problem costs in money, time, risk, or people. The call to action must be a specific next step with an owner, not "let's think about it".

### Story — building emotion and holding attention

`Context → challenge → journey → outcome → lesson`

Use when the audience is tired, sceptical, or has heard the informational version already. One protagonist, one turning point. The lesson must connect explicitly back to the audience's own situation, or it stays a nice anecdote.

### Elimination — a contested topic with several camps

`Problem → the available options → limits of each → recommendation`

Use when the room holds people who already favour different answers. Naming each option fairly, including the one you will reject, buys the credibility that makes the recommendation land. Never strawman the option someone in the room champions.

## Ordering the body sections

Pick one and stay in it. Mixing orders mid-talk is where audiences get lost.

- **Priority** — most important first. Good for executives and short slots; they may leave with only section one.
- **Theme** — parallel topics of similar weight. Signpost the count early ("three areas").
- **Chronology** — history, roadmap, process walkthrough. Anchor each step with a date or stage number.
- **Cause and effect** — when the audience needs to accept *why* before *what*.
- **Problem and solution** — when each section is its own pain point with its own answer.

## Transitions

Every transition does four things. Write it out; do not improvise it.

1. Summarise what just finished
2. Restate why it mattered
3. Announce what comes next
4. Name the link between the two

Reusable shapes:

- "So that's [X] — the takeaway being [why it mattered]. That raises the obvious question: [next section], which is where I'm going now."
- "We've just seen [X] costs us [impact]. Knowing that, the next thing to look at is [Y]."
- "That covers [first area]. [Second area] is the other half of the same problem, and it's what the next ten minutes are about."
- Signposting the end: "Before I open it up for questions, let me pull the three threads together."

**Group presentations.** The outgoing speaker: summarises their own part, hands over by name, and states what the next person will cover. "That's the data side. [Name] is going to take you through what it means for operations." Rehearse the handovers specifically — they are the most visibly under-prepared moments in most group talks.

## Worked example — "AI in Action" workshop

Brief: SME owners and operators; know AI exists, have not put it into a workflow; goal is that they leave with one process they can automate and a plan to do it.

Structure chosen — **problem–solution with an embedded demo and hands-on practice**:

`SME problem → three business processes → AI + CRM system → live demo → applied exercise → action plan`

| # | Section | Main point | So what | Min |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The SME problem | Small teams lose their week to repeatable work | This is your week, not a hypothetical | 10 |
| 2 | Three business processes | Lead intake, customer follow-up, reporting — the three that repeat most | Pick which of the three is bleeding most for you | 20 |
| 3 | AI + CRM system | How the pieces connect into one system | You do not need engineers to assemble this | 20 |
| 4 | Live demo | Watch one process run end to end | Proof it works, not a promise it could | 25 |
| 5 | Applied exercise | Map your own process on the worksheet | You now have your own, not mine | 30 |
| 6 | Action plan | The first step, this week, with a named owner | Leave with a commitment, not inspiration | 15 |

Note the shape: the demo sits *after* the audience has recognised their own problem in section 1 and chosen their target in section 2, and the workshop closes on commitment rather than summary. A workshop breaks 10–20–30 on slide count and duration — it should — but each individual slide still holds one idea in 30pt+ type.
